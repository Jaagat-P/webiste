a walk in fidithe world is malleable (as supported by math and milk)

I recently went for a walk with a friend in FiDi. No, not the one in NY. The one in SF (I didn't even know FiDi was a thing in SF until recently).

I enjoyed the walk because I felt grounded in the moment. More importantly, I know I enjoyed it because I completely lost track of where I was walking and what time it was. ~45 minutes felt like just a few minutes.

The walk was kinda overstimulating: financial-quarter-zips rushing home to their sweetgreen meals (I presume), youngsters strolling and hanging out, folks hastily walking from traffic light to traffic light, cars eagerly honking to assert their egos, kids jaywalking for the sake of it, mothers strolling their precious little ones, etc.

But none of this really entered my attention (except the little ones). Although it was overstimulating with all sorts of beep beeps and chitter chatters, I felt so engrossed in the conversation. Within a few minutes, the financial-quarter-zips, youngsters, cars, etc. left my focus (albeit I was doing my regular LiDAR scans to ensure no maniac would hit us or do something goofy).

The conversation felt natural and genuine. From books to black coffee to dirty chai (I didn't know this was a thing) to travel (and fun history such as the Maya) to future plans to birthdays to LLMs (mandatory honorable mention) to Texas, I enjoyed every second. Also, walking to an ice cream store to not buy ice cream was ironically fun and I don't know why.

A lot stuck with me during the walk. Most notably, during the walk, one statement that stuck was "the world is malleable." Now, I was already pretty engrossed in the conversation. But at that moment, I felt like my neurons were synaptically awakened (I also may have been coffee-deprived): it's the equivalent feeling of a dog licking your face. And you seldom hear it from other people, which is why it felt special. Don't get me wrong — I genuinely enjoyed talking about books, Texas, etc. too.

The belief of the world being malleable is something that's been ingrained within me for as long as I can remember. It's a belief that I will always live by.

A mathematical (and maybe quirky) analogy of this is a function. You have inputs and you have outputs. Take, for example, f(x, y, z) = some output, where x, y, and z live in an input domain. When you're fed those inputs, your outputs (happiness, joy, wealth, love, thoughts, etc.) are constrained by a range (and implicitly by your inputs).

What a lot of people perhaps don't realize is that those inputs are never fixed. You can rotate them, divide them, multiply them, exponentiate them, etc. to yield different outputs. And more often than not, manipulating your input domain means placing yourself in different environments: surrounding yourself with different people, enforcing good habits, and identifying your purpose, intentions, and wants. Note that this so-called function isn't fixed. It's malleable too. Another analogy is milk. There's oat milk, whole milk, 2%, half-and-half, 1%, skim, etc. Milk, such a primitive ingredient and essential (i.e., a domain element) can be "multiplied" to yield different outputs such as (you guessed it) dirty chai, coffee, or hot chocolate (and on a wider spectrum, cheese, milk, bread, etc.).

Now, perhaps these aren't the best analogies, but the point is that everything around us, whether it's this keyboard I'm tapping on, the cafe across my street, today's outdated medical system, the financial systems we depend upon, the food supply chain, etc. — it's not fixed. It can be changed. We just settle for it because it seems established and, ah I despise when people say this, because "that's how things are / have always been."

Most people deem such things to be fixed because it seems hard to challenge what's already been established, what history and humanity has supported for decades and more. It's been there for so long and so it has to be right? (hint: no, it doesn't).

I fear a world where we all settle for what's already seemingly established and don't challenge prior views, take action, and define the new "conventional." Yes, we can design medicines within days, paperwork doesn't have to take years, you don't need a PhD to do research, you can genuinely learn anything you want to, etc.

I have always viewed it as you either change the world (I know, cliche) or you let the world change you, the latter of which in you succumb to preexisting beliefs or systems that you could have otherwise challenged to further benefit humanity. Most people, it seems, don't realize this until they're older (or less younger I guess), when time is constrained by what we all value most: health, time, and love. Our objectives shift and when we're young, we shouldn't settle for less: we should unwaveringly shape the future with utmost conviction.

There's so much value that humans can create and welcome into the world, which often comes from embracing the fact that people can welcome beneficial changes if they hold the conviction to do so. A conviction so strong that the noise of others' opinions, your classic "I don't think this is possible…" dissipates into nothing, and that instinct inside of you just never settles for less.

Humans, no smarter or creative than us, established such history. It's our choice to change it or live with it.

The Maya made significant contributions to astronomy, calendars, and mathematics. They didn't settle for what they were just given or the situation they were in. They tracked the movements of the Sun, Moon, planets, and especially Venus. They developed the 260-day Tzolk'in calendar, the 365-day Haab' calendar, and the Long Count system for dates across centuries. They didn't simply accept the knowledge available to them; they created sophisticated systems for countless generations ahead.

Similarly, in Yemen, Sufi communities roasted beans and brewed coffee, setting the stage for what you could call black coffee. For Sweetgreen, a few Georgetown students felt frustrated with unhealthy fast food and wanted fast and quick fresh food; they didn't settle for the status quo. And for dirty chai, supposedly a barista mistakenly (not sure) mixed an espresso shot into a chai latte in Covent Garden, London.

The walk in FiDi was fun. It's one I won't forget.

TSR: Temporal Subspace Routing

Note: This post is a work in progress — I'll be adding plots and more content soon. Also, if you have idle GPUs and found this blog interesting, I would very gratefully put them to work (these experiments are done on remaining Stanford Modal credits).

In this blog, we explore the idea of learning from delayed feedback without storing the whole past. I.e., when feedback arrives late, does a recurrent model really need to store and revisit its entire history to learn from it?

The standard method here, backpropagation through time (BPTT), does that. It stores the computation performed at every step and, once the target or reward arrives, it follows the computation backward to determine which of those events mattered.

But, as humans, we don't quite have to backpropagate through every single instance in our life to make decisions? I mean, last time I checked, when I ordered a cheese croissant at Golden Goat at 8:26am on a Thursday morning, I didn't have to backpropagate through checkpoints in my life where I learned to speak, move, interact, smile at the barista, systematically move my hand to pay, and well, devour that nice cheese croissant (highly recommend; it's there on Wed and Thu).

Now, I am not arguing that these machine learning methods have to be exactly like humans, but it begs the question: how can we make these more efficient?

The problem with BPTT

BPTT is an accurate method, but the problem here is that long sequences make it expensive. Its memory use grows with the sequence length, and a late reward can trigger a long backward pass.

We introduce the idea of temporal subspace routing (TSR), which takes a different approach.

While the sequence is running, TSR maintains a compact summary of how those earlier events can influence a future loss. When the feedback arrives, it uses that summary instead of revisiting the complete sequence!

Why is this important? Well, first, data efficiency. Second, it goes back to the idea of a delayed credit problem. Suppose a model observes an important signal and receives the corresponding target 4,096 steps later. The full sequence would contain \(4{,}096 + 1 = 4{,}097\) steps as one step contains the cue and the other 4,096 steps come before the outcome.

BPTT in this case would build a temporal computation graph during these steps. Once the target arrives, it performs a backward sweep through this graph.

There's also the version of truncated BPTT, or TBPTT, which reduces the cost by cutting the graph periodically (e.g., TBPTT-16, for e.g., propagates the credit through only the most recent 16 steps, which is cheap, but an event from thousands of steps earlier is normally outside the credit window).

Activation-checkpointed BPTT also stores the selected states and reconstructs the missing computation during the backward pass. This approach saves memory while preserving the exact gradient (but the reconstruction makes the response after feedback considerably slower).

Temporal subspace routing

TSR instead tries to compress the relevant temporal influence as the sequence unfolds. Now, what does that even mean and how does this even work?

Let \(h_t\) be the hidden state of a recurrent model. The local Jacobian is

\[ J_t = \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}} \]

This describes how the previous hidden state affects the current one.

A product of these Jacobians, thus, describes how an old state can affect a state much later in the sequence (this product can be big, but in our experiments, we observe that this long range influence often becomes concentrated in a small number of directions after the model learns a stable memory strategy).

The forward TSR method tracks a rank-\(r\) basis \(U_t\) for those directions:

\[ J_t U_{t-1} = U_t R_t \]

The matrix \(R_t\) describes how the tracked subspace changes from one step to the next one.

We also simultaneously maintain a compressed parameter-eligibility trace:

\[ S_t = R_t S_{t-1} + U_t^\top L_t \]

where \(L_t\) represents the local effect of the model's parameters on the hidden state. The trace is target free (i.e., it is constructed before the model knows the future reward) and records how earlier parameter changes could affect a later hidden state.

Once this outcome does arrive, TSR combines the terminal error (the terminal error is the error signal you get at the end of the sequence once the target/reward finally arrives) with the trace:

\[ \widehat{\nabla_\theta L} \approx (g_T^\top U_T)\, S_T \]

where \(g_T\) is the final loss gradient with respect to the terminal hidden state.

This then produces a long range gradient estimate without having to run backward through the entire sequence.

We choose to keep the recent credit exact (i.e., TSR compresses credit from the distant past).

The long lived credit often becomes low dimensional because the model learns a small and stable memory channel; the recent credit remains more complicated because this includes the local computation, gates, readout behavior (i.e., short-term interactions).

The hybrid TSR method thus uses a compressed TSR credit for the distant past and an exact BPTT through the final 16 steps.

We came to this design decision through a bunch of experiments that we later talk about (i.e., we aren't trying to compress every gradient equally, but rather compress the part that remains structured across long delays and keep the more complicated local part exact).

Does rank track remembered information?

A natural question to also ask here is if rank follows the amount of remembered information? A small output space can make terminal gradients look low-rank for trivial reasons, so we built a more controlled experiment.

This was more a capacity decision and not an optimization decision (i.e., a more ideal and practical algorithm would discover the correct subspace when the model is changing).

Preserving data efficiency

On the topic of data-efficiency, we next ask whether compressed temporal credit could preserve the value of these labeled examples.

A GRU here first learned to remember two important cues! We then adapted it to a new nonlinear query using a limited number of labels. This comparison included the full BPTT run, a low-rank oracle, practical forward TSR, and terminal only learning. The low rank oracle is just an idealized version of TSR that is told the best low-dimensional credit directions using the exact BPTT gradient (the goal of this is to figure out that if we can get the optimal subspace, would this idea of low-rank credit be enough). The practical forward TSR is the TSR method that has to discover and update the useful low rank credit subspace itself during the forward pass. Terminal only learning is when the model learns only from what happens at the final stage (i.e., it doesn't send credit back to the important events from much earlier in the sequence so it ignores this delayed credit idea).

Making TSR fast

So, after a long walk at this point, I decided to pivot and ask: TSR can learn, but how do we actually make this thing efficient and useful? Like, how do we make this Max Verstappen (or <insert_favorite_F1_driver_idk> fast, or even blazingly faster)?

Vroom vroom. The next step was to make TSR faster! We ran some profiling and showed that most of the runtime actually came from repeatedly transporting a large eligibility tensor:

\[ S \leftarrow RS \]

REEE(call) that this eligibility tensor is just a big running data thingy that stores how each parameter's earlier effect could still influence the current hidden state.

We make a few adjustments here to make TSR cheaper!

Modal transport: full method retains interactions between every pair of credit modes; so, we keep only per-mode scaling, reducing the main transport cost from quadratic to linear in the trace rank. So, basically, instead of tracking how every credit direction interacts with every other direction, we track each direction on its own.

Adapter eligibility: we restricted distant eligibility to parameters involved in writing information into the memory (stopped maintaining an expensive general trace for parameter blocks that contributed little useful long range credit). Basically, we only track long range credit for the parameters that actually write info into the memory and not every parameter.

Chunked scans: we no longer change the full eligibility tensor at every step, but now accumulate local contributions and contract them once per fixed chunk. On the continuous-memory benchmark, this reduced runtime from about 52s to 21s without changing 3 seed accuracy! I.e., we don't update the huge eligibility tensor every single timestep, but rather accumulate changes and update it once per chunky chunk.

Less frequent basis refreshes: We don't enforce a complete QR factorization at every step and instead use cheaper normalization between periodic full basis refreshes. I.e., we don't do that expensive re-orthogonalization of the credit directions at every step (only occasionally now). This QR factorization was used to keep the tracked basis \(U_t\) well behaved (i.e., orthogonal here).

Recent window: At rank 8, adding an exact 16-step BPTT window raised the mean \(R^2\) from approximately 0.834 to 0.949. Furthermore, at rank 24, the best hybrid TSR configuration reached \(R^2\) = 0.9898 (compared with 0.9939 for BPTT). I.e., we use the normal exact BPTT for the last 16 steps where the credit is more complicated (this improved accuracy).

Boundary reuse: The hybrid implementation originally replayed an old prefix to reconstruct the state at the beginning of its exact window. We reused the state already computed during the TSR pass. I.e., TSR had already gotten the hidden state to start the final 16-step BPTT window, so instead of just replaying the earlier sequence to get that state, we save it.

The mean runtime fell from 49.00 to 37.12 seconds and every final score was the same.

These improvements made TSR much faster, but BPTT was still faster on the continuous memory benchmark (recall this is the synthetic task where the GRU has to remember continuous numerical values across a long delay and use them later). You can almost think of this as waking up in the morning and your dog running to you and barking eight numbers at you (in Martha Speaks, the dogs can speak ok?) and at the end of the day, you have to remember them!

Before and after the feedback

Then, I also became interested in understanding the work that was performed before and after a delayed outcome.

During the preparation phase, the TSR method processes the sequence and builds its own target free trace (it retains the trace and a short recent suffix, i.e., the last few timesteps of the sequence). The target free trace here is just a running summary of past parameter effects built from before knowing the reward. During the finish phase, the target becomes available and TSR combines the target error with the stored trace and differentiates through a recent suffix.

The BPTT method can also process the sequence before the target arrives, but it must retain the temporal graph and later run its complete backward pass (ew).

At 16,385 total steps, using five paired seeds on Modal L4 GPUs, we obtained the following:

MethodFeedback to gradientRetained memoryComplete update
Full BPTT13.05 s1,620 MiB23.61 s
Checkpointed BPTT, chunk 25628.42 s85 MiB41.18 s
Hybrid TSR25.3 ms105 MiB34.82 s

Compared to the full BPTT method, the hybrid TSR (i.e., TSR for distant past and exact BPTT for the most recent few steps) produced the gradient about 515 times faster after feedback and retained 93.5% less memory (albeit it was still slower over the complete update because its forward preparation was expensive). I.e., TSR spends a lot of time before the feedback building and transporting the trace, and is fast once that feedback arrives. For BPTT, it has a cheaper forward pass, but this spends a lot of time after feedback doing the backward pass.

Compared with the checkpointed BPTT, TSR used slightly more retained memory but was 15.4% faster overall end-to-end and more than a thousand times faster after feedback!

This established a useful latency memory trade off, which I felt compelled to look further into.

MiniGrid: delayed decisions

We then decided to experiment with MiniGrid (where our first experiment attempted full navigation from terminal rewards). Before comparing TSR from BPTT, we first did a quick sanity check to see if the BPTT learner could solve the task reliably, which it couldn't: after 38,400 episodes, the BPTT actor-critic baseline reached 0.512 greedy success, below our predefined 0.70 threshold. So, we then isolated the delayed branch decision from perception, navigation, and exploration to keep this more controlled.

The controlled MiniGrid decision task we established included an environment that determines: 1) whether the initial cue is a key or a ball, 2) which object appears on each branch, and 3) which branch is correct. A branch here is just one of the possible paths that a model can choose at the decision point!

We convert these facts into an eight dimensional semantic representation. The model sees the cue at the first step, receives the decision context throughout the delay, and then predicts the correct branch at the end.

The navigation component is removed from the learning problem (i.e., this is a controlled delayed decision benchmark that we build from MiniGrid states, not the full MiniGrid navigation).

A delay of 4,096 creates a 4,097 step sequence as we have one cue step and 4,096 delayed steps.

Here, an ordinary GRU solved the task at delay 64 but was near chance at delay 1,024, even with BPTT (the hidden state itself had forgotten the cue before the target arrived).

Thus, we gave every method the same cue write architecture. Now, half of the 32-unit GRU was initialized as a slow memory channel. And, the cue event is written into that channel, allowing the model to store the relevant information and carry it across the delay (channel changes very slowly so the cue can likely stay for a longer range). I.e., about 16 normal units for regular computation and about 16 slow-memory units designed to preserve the cue.

This now separated two nice problems (well, not so nice, but nicely separated I guess): the first was state memory (can the network preserve the cue?) and the second was credit memory (can learning connect the final outcome to the earlier cue writing parameters)?

Once the state memory problem was controlled, both the BPTT and TSR could solve delays of 1,024 and 4,096 (the original rank 16 TSR matched the accuracy but it remained kinda slow compared to BPTT; its trace was more general than the binary task asked for). I.e., the binary task here means that there are two possible choices.

Fast TSR

So, at this point, the latest fast TSR configuration uses a distant rank of 2 (instead of 16), an old eligibility only for the cue writing map, exact BPTT just for those final 16 steps (think hybrid approach), modal chunks of 256 steps (i.e., TSR groups the sequence into blocks of 256 timesteps), a full basis refresh every 64 steps (between we use cheaper normalization). We call this Fast TSR!

BPTT and Fast TSR use the same 32-unit GRU, optimizer, training examples, and paired seeds as you'd expect.

Rank 2 is a task informed choice we establish here. The outcome is binary and from our earliest experiments, this showed that useful long range rank follows the number of persistent quantities (i.e., don't assume this rank to be universal).

At a 4,096-step delay, both methods were trained on 4,096 examples using five paired seeds.

The Fast TSR matched BPTT's final accuracy, reduced the total training time by 5.6%, and used 88.3% less peak training memory!

So, this establishes matched accuracy with fixed training, but this doesn't tell us that Fast TSR learns from fewer examples (i.e., data efficiency!!).

The takeaway

What should we take away from all of this? On a controlled long-horizon task, this Fast TSR matches BPTT's accuracy while reducing total time, retained memory, and the delayed feedback latency (now, it's worth noting that we had to do some tuning/searching to get this to kinda work, which in the ideal goal of data efficiency, we could further generalize this). We also observe a few other trends such as how long lived credit becomes low dimensional as recurrent models learn stable memory dynamics and how a carefully specialized Fast TSR approach can cross the full BPTT's time and memory usage.

We ultimately arrive at the conclusion that when long lived credit occupies a small, organized subspace, a recurrent model perhaps doesn't need to retain and revisit its complete past to learn from that delayed feedback.
